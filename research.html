My current PhD work is on energy system optimization models, the tools planners use to figure out how a city, region, or country should build and run its power grid over the next few decades to hit cost, environmental or reliability targets.

Temoa

An open-source model that simulates how an energy system could evolve over decades, used to compare long-term investment and policy choices. I'm a collaborator on the project; a publication is in progress.

GNN/GAT & Gradient Boosting for Transmission Expansion & Line Switching

Testing ML methods to find where to build new transmission lines, and which existing lines to switch off to relieve congestion, checked against full AC power flow. The expansion models are path-aware, so they judge the feasibility of a line corridor on land use, geographic, and environmental factors from OpenStreetMap. Tested on the synthetic Texas ACTIVSg2000 grid.

An imaginary grid: these five nodes sit at the real locations of five cities across four countries, but the power plants, the transmission lines between them, and every MW figure are invented for this demo, not the actual Colombian grid. Two hydro reservoirs you can dial up or down sit at Panama City and at Ibarra, Ecuador, both included mainly to give the layout some breathing room. Punto Fijo, on Venezuela's windy Paraguaná Peninsula, hosts a fixed wind injection (no curtailment modeled), and Bogotá and Cali, Colombia's two biggest cities after Medellín, are the load centers, tied together by five made-up lines, each with its own thermal limit. Dial the hydro plants down and see whether the rest of the grid can still cover demand. Turn the Panama City–Cali line off and watch flow reroute onto the Ibarra–Cali line, maybe past what it can carry, the same kind of tradeoff the line-switching work above looks for automatically. Each plant also carries a made-up $/MWh cost, the Panama City–Cali line has its own wheeling fee, and any shortfall or surplus Bogotá quietly trades with the rest of the grid is priced too, all tallied east of the map, so you can go looking for the cheapest way to meet demand yourself.

These five nodes sit at their real coordinates (Bogotá and Cali in Colombia, Panama City in Panama, Ibarra just over the border in Ecuador, and Punto Fijo on Venezuela's Paraguaná Peninsula); everything else, the plants, the lines, the capacities, the dispatch numbers, is invented for this demo and not a reproduction of Colombia's actual grid. Flows are solved with a linearized (DC) power flow: bus angles from line reactances, no losses or reactive power, the same kind of fast approximation that gets checked against a full AC power flow in the work above. Bogotá doubles as the model's reference bus, so it silently makes up any gap between the dialed-in hydro, the fixed wind, and both cities' demand, standing in for the rest of Colombia's interconnected grid.

Before my PhD, I worked on making solar panel data more useful: panels generate messy, unlabeled power readings, and small problems like shade from a nearby tree, clouds, accumulated dust, or circuitry problems can cut how much energy they produce. Each of these losses has different impacts, so we can diagnose them from just looking at the power generated by the panel. This work builds tools to catch and quantify those losses automatically.

A single panel's power output over a day already tells you a lot. A clean signal traces a smooth arc as the sun rises and sets. Soiling (dust, pollen, grime) quietly shaves that whole arc down. A passing cloud instead carves a sharp dip at one moment, then recovers. Separating those two signatures from raw power data, without needing a label telling you which is which, is the core problem this line of work solves.

Modeled from a clear-sky irradiance curve (pvlib, Austin TX, Jun 21), not a measured panel, with the cloud, soiling, and obstruction effects layered on top to show the signal shapes this work learns to tell apart.

Development and validation of a machine learning system to identify reflux events

Trained a machine learning model to automatically flag acid reflux events in 24-hour esophageal monitoring data, cutting down hours of manual review by doctors. We tried deep neural nets, LSTMs, and even some Fourier analysis methods. Transformers resulted the best overall.
